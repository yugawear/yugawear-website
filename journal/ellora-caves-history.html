What are the Ellora Caves?

The Ellora Caves are a remarkable complex of rock-cut monasteries, temples and sanctuaries in Maharashtra. Across 34 principal caves, Buddhist, Brahmanical and Jain traditions exist within the same extraordinary landscape.

The monuments were excavated over several centuries, creating an uninterrupted architectural record that demonstrates the technical, artistic and cultural achievements of ancient India.

Some architecture is assembled. Ellora was carved.

Along the basalt cliffs of the Charanandri hills in Maharashtra, generations of artisans transformed solid volcanic rock into monasteries, sanctuaries, temples and monumental sculptural environments.

What makes Ellora extraordinary is not simply its scale. The complex preserves monuments associated with Buddhism, Brahmanism and Jainism, created across several centuries and placed within one continuous architectural landscape.

01

A landscape carved over centuries.

UNESCO describes Ellora as a sequence of 34 monasteries and temples extending for more than two kilometres along a high basalt cliff in Maharashtra.

The rock-cut activity developed in several phases. The earliest Buddhist caves belong broadly to the 5th–8th centuries. The Brahmanical group followed between the 7th and 10th centuries, while the Jain caves belong broadly to the 9th–12th centuries.

This chronology means Ellora is not a single monument created at one moment. It is an architectural landscape built through successive acts of excavation, adaptation and artistic expression.

02

Architecture without construction.

Ellora represents one of the most ambitious forms of rock-cut architecture in India.

Instead of bringing stone to a building site and assembling it, artisans worked directly into the natural basalt cliff. The building and the material were therefore inseparable.

Monastic halls, pillared spaces, sanctuaries, courtyards and sculptural compositions were progressively excavated from the rock.

When the building and the stone become one, architecture becomes excavation.

This process demanded extraordinary spatial planning. Every column, passage, wall and sculptural surface had to emerge from the same mass of stone while maintaining the intended architectural composition.

03

Kailasa, the temple carved downward.

The most famous monument at Ellora is Cave 16, the Kailasa Temple.

Unlike a conventional temple that is constructed piece by piece, Kailasa was excavated from the surrounding rock. The result is one of the world's most remarkable monolithic architectural achievements.

UNESCO identifies Cave 16 as the largest monolithic temple in the Ellora complex and describes it as a culmination of Indian rock-cut architecture.

The temple translates architectural forms normally associated with constructed buildings into a single mass of excavated stone. Pillars, halls, sculptural compositions and monumental forms were all produced through the removal of material.

04

Three traditions, one landscape.

One of Ellora's defining qualities is the presence of monuments associated with three major religious traditions.

The Buddhist caves include monasteries and sanctuaries reflecting the development of Mahayana and later Vajrayana traditions.

The Brahmanical group contains some of the complex's most ambitious architectural compositions, including the Kailasa Temple.

The later Jain caves are known for their refined sculptural treatment, delicate details and monuments associated with the Digambara tradition.

05

Stone becomes storytelling.

At Ellora, sculpture is not separate from architecture.

Figures, deities, narrative compositions and ornamental details are integrated directly into walls, pillars and architectural surfaces.

Among the most celebrated compositions is the depiction of Ravana attempting to lift Mount Kailasa at Cave 16.

The sculptural language changes across the different religious groups, but the underlying principle remains consistent: architecture provides the structure through which stories, beliefs and visual symbols are experienced.

Why does Ellora feel so monumental?

Ellora's visual power comes from the tension between mass and detail.

Massive rock surfaces establish a sense of permanence, while columns, carvings and sculptural details introduce rhythm and human scale.

The experience also changes constantly. Moving through a cave changes the relationship between darkness and light, enclosure and openness, rough rock and finely carved surfaces.

This combination turns the architecture into an experience rather than simply an object to observe.

07

From rock-cut monument to World Heritage.

The Ellora Caves were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1983 under criteria (i), (iii) and (vi).

UNESCO recognises Ellora as a unique artistic achievement and a technological accomplishment, particularly because of the extraordinary excavation of the Kailasa Temple.

The site is also significant for the way Buddhist, Brahmanical and Jain monuments exist within the same cultural landscape, reflecting a history of coexistence and religious interaction.

The Ellora Caves are managed by the Archaeological Survey of India, with conservation and management focused on protecting the site's architectural, sculptural and environmental integrity.

Ellora Caves FAQ.

What are the Ellora Caves?

The Ellora Caves are a monumental complex of rock-cut Buddhist, Brahmanical and Jain monasteries and temples in Maharashtra.

Where are the Ellora Caves located?

The Ellora Caves are located near Verul in Maharashtra, within the Charanandri hills.

How many caves are there at Ellora?

The UNESCO World Heritage property contains 34 principal caves and monuments extending for more than two kilometres along the basalt cliff.

What is the Kailasa Temple?

Kailasa Temple is Cave 16 at Ellora. It is the largest monolithic temple in the complex and was excavated directly from the surrounding basalt.
